How to read this. Jakal Hierophant is a character in the Greyhawk world, played at the keyboard by Marc. This is his story so far, built from what the world itself kept: Jakal’s own memories, the memories of the people who met him, and the log of every prompt and every answer the narrator gave him.

Words set in blue are Jakal’s: what Marc typed for him, with capitals and spelling mended (and, once or twice, a garbled clause straightened), or what the narrator made of it in the moment. Every other quoted line is spoken by the person named, exactly as the record has it. Each part is headed by the world’s own count of days, the narrator’s clock, followed by the date Marc played it (UTC); the two do not run alike, because the world’s clock turns on its players’ turns. I used only what Jakal himself saw, heard and remembered, and nobody else’s private memory. What I added is the weather of the telling: no event here is invented, and nobody says anything they did not say.

Part OneA Scholar at the Welcome Wench

World days 1–2 · played 26 September

He came in at the tail of the evening, slight of frame and tall in dark green robes, with a wooden staff in one hand, a dagger at his belt and a set of balanced throwing darts at his hip. In his own first account of himself he was “freshly graduated, schooled in the rudiments of arcane casting and basic healing rites, driven by an eager curiosity to uncover nature’s herbal lore and expand my knowledge.” He had walked into Hommlet with a head full of theory and a purse of modest weight.

The Welcome Wench was loud with mutton and pipe smoke. At the oak counter a burly man with forearms like rolled hams was drying a pewter tankard. Jakal went straight up to him.

I go up to the bar and introduce myself to the bartender. I’d like a mug of ale and whatever food they serve here. I engage the bartender in small talk to see what’s going on in Hommlet.

Marc, as Jakal — the first thing he ever did

The innkeeper gave his name as Ostler Gundigoot, set down dark ale and a trencher of roast fowl, and rested his forearms on the counter. “A warm welcome to you, Master Jakal.” The village was peaceful enough, he said, under the eye of Canon Terjon, with Lord Burne’s new stone keep rising on the eastern ridge, “yet a few too many armed wanderers have drifted in lately, asking after the ruined moathouse out in the marshes.”

A tipsy woodcutter had swayed up to the counter beside him. “Elmo’s the name, Elmo Thistlewood,” he said. “I fell trees for whoever pays an’ drink what’s left over.” Jakal answered cagily.

Jakal’s my name, and I might be — I might be just passing through. What of the old ruins?

Elmo leaned in, grinning lopsidedly, and dropped his voice. “That old moathouse down the east track? Nasty place, scholar, full of bog vermin and cellar creepers.” Giant frogs lurked in the moat weeds, he said, and bad sorts had been seen sniffing round the tumbled walls after dark. “Ain’t no place to wander alone unless you’ve got stout steel beside you, or someone who knows where the sinkholes hide.” He swayed as he said it, though his gaze stayed steady on Jakal’s staff.

Across the room a lean young man in a scholar’s collar had looked up sharply from his cup at the word ruins. In a corner a dark, narrow-eyed man named Turuko exchanged a glance with his armoured companion Kobort. Ostler went on drying his tankard and quietly shook his head.

The young man was Spugnoir of Dyvers, a journeyman arcanist with ink-stained fingers. By the end of the night he and Jakal had shaken hands on the only terms either of them could afford to offer: they would split all coin equally, and share and copy any magical scroll or grimoire they found. Jakal told him what he knew. “I share with Spug what spells I know,” Marc wrote, and Spugnoir, hearing a first-circle repertoire that began with Sleep and Magic Missile, pronounced it “a balanced foundation.” Sleep wanted sand and rose petals. Two spells a day, and a few restorative rites besides, was all the novice could carry.

Ostler, leaning over the bar, had already quietly warned them not to hire anyone tonight on ale and a handshake, and to ask at the church or the keep first. Spugnoir’s plan answered it, and he gave it in an undertone across the counter: “Gundigoot speaks sense. Master Burne at the stone keep is an accomplished mage of great standing — an introduction there might grant us valuable counsel, if not access to deeper arcane lore. And the Cuthbertines at the church know what foul things linger in the fen. I propose we secure a room for the night, rest our minds, and visit the tower and the church at first light before we commit to any company.” Jakal took the heavy iron key to the upstairs room.

In the morning the same two men were at the hearth, watching. “Those two watching us last night and at breakfast,” Jakal told Spugnoir on the road, “seem rather shady.” It was the first thing he said about Turuko and Kobort, and all month he would turn out to be right about them.

· · ·

The keep gate was shut to everyone until noon, so they went to the church. On its knoll the Church of St. Cuthbert smelled of tallow and old stone, and a young acolyte named Calmert greeted them as two mages come looking for the Cudgel, which is what the faithful call their saint. Jakal paid twenty-five gold pieces into the offering bowl for a crystal vial of holy water. Canon Terjon spoke up from the chancel, telling them to go with honest steel and not idlers from the taproom, and to come back and report what they saw. Then Jakal did something that set the shape of a good half of his month. He asked for company.

I give a donation for the holy water. Would any of your acolytes be willing to accompany us as representatives of the faith?

Calmert glanced back toward the chancel. From the shadows Canon Terjon, arms folded, gave a firm, measured nod. “With the Canon’s permission,” said Calmert, one hand on the pommel of his mace, “I would willingly accompany you to root out whatever filth festers in the fens, provided the Cudgel receives a full share of any recovered spoils and an honest accounting of deeds.”

“It is a blessing just to have you with us, Calmert,” Jakal said. “Now let us find ourselves a good sword arm.”

The Canon had a suggestion for that. Brother Smyth at the smithy on the mill road, he said, “is a faithful son of the Cudgel. He knows every strong arm in this village and which of them can be trusted.” Smyth turned out to be a sooty, sensible man who offered to close up the worn left shoulder of Calmert’s chain mail while they shopped. Jakal asked, hopefully, whether he and Spugnoir might use any of the armour on the racks.

“A wizard in plate,” said Smyth, “is a drowned dog in a pond.”

The swords and shields would only spoil their dweomers and trip their feet, the smith explained, but cold iron spikes, a prying bar, a pair of balanced throwing knives — those weighed little and served any man in a tight cellar. Jakal paid seven gold for two throwing knives, ten spikes and a crowbar; Spugnoir, who by his own cheerful admission was “a poor mule,” took the knives and spikes and left the bar for Calmert. Smyth, who had already let slip that the tipsy woodcutter only played the drunkard, and had an honest arm and a deep knowledge of the fens, set Jakal right on the name. Jakal had half-remembered it as Otto. “Elmo’s the name you’re looking for, not Otto.”

They found him nursing small beer at the Wench, and he dropped the act in a whisper. He had put on the sot for the room, he said; his terms were an equal share, food and ale, and the last word on turning back. He asked them to keep quiet until the road. Jakal agreed to all of it and named a place and an hour. “Let’s meet at the east track tomorrow at sunrise,” he said. “Dawn, then,” said Elmo. “Now go, before the whole room hears you.”

At noon the guard Oswin Hale let them into the stone tower. Master Burne and his captain, Rufus, received them in a study with drafting tables, and Burne said what mages say. He would trade spells with the young scholars, but only for lost dweomers recovered from the ruin, or proof of who led the brigands there. He warned them of the old Temple cultists who had first built the place. Rufus, more practically, offered a bounty of twenty gold for intelligence on the moathouse’s occupants.

At midday, over stew, Jakal and Spugnoir reviewed their gear and their spells. Later, upstairs, Jakal decided what to do about the men at the hearth, who had watched every move he made. “Tomorrow we need to leave quietly and unseen through a back door,” he told Spugnoir. “Those lowlifes downstairs are watching us way too closely.” Before dawn he gathered their supplies, left the room key on the washstand, and led his partner down the back stairs and out through the kitchen postern into the mist.

On the east track, in the grey, Calmert and Elmo were waiting. Jakal asked each of them to name what they could do. “I share my spells,” he said, “and ask each of the others to declare their skills so we all know each other’s capabilities.” It was, in miniature, a habit he kept: he wanted everyone to know who they were to each other.

Part TwoThe Moathouse, First Blood

World day 3 · played 26–27 September

The moat was scummed and stinking. Bubbles rose near the far bank, and for a moment two huge pale eyes surfaced and sank again. Jakal had been told about the frogs.

Elmo had set the order: himself ahead, then Calmert with his painted shield, then the two mages. Jakal looked at the black water and made his first tactical suggestion, which was to stop pretending it was not there. “Should we draw them out? I don’t like them staying in the water. Better to deal with them now, I say.”

“Aye,” said Elmo. “Better on the stones than under ’em.”

Calmert baited the nearer frog with the Cudgel painted on his shield, and the water erupted. Jakal, staying behind and to the side of the woodsman, threw dart after dart. One took a frog in the throat. “Keep back from the gullet!” Elmo shouted. A second dart glanced off the ridge of a skull. When the creature came up the bank Jakal closed and cracked its skull with his staff, shouting for Spugnoir to help, and Elmo finished it with the axe.

Inside the courtyard they stopped. “Let’s proceed carefully,” Jakal said. “I’ve heard there’s brigands holed up here, and their eyes could be on us right now. Not to mention all kinds of other nasty creatures.” Elmo agreed in his own way. Drawing them out would only give their archers the yard to shoot across. “Better we slip inside the shadows of the great hall first. Catch ’em while they’re still eating mutton.”

· · ·

The great hall had lost half its roof. Fallen timbers lay across cracked flagstones, and in the dim light by an iron brazier three men in leather armour sat at their ease. Two tossed dice across a barrel top. The third, by a pillar, was working a bowstring onto its notch. Behind a heap of masonry, four adventurers crouched and breathed.

Jakal could put all three to sleep, but it would spend one of the two spells he could cast in a day. He whispered it to the others. “I could sleep them all, but that uses one of my two spells per day. Thoughts?”

Elmo did not look away from the brazier. “Three men asleep can’t shoot back. Spend it. The day’s long, but it’s longer with an arrow in you.”

Calmert nodded once behind his shield. “Put them down and we’ll bind them. Sleeping men can still answer the Captain’s questions.”

Spugnoir touched his sleeve. “I’ll keep Burning Hands back for whatever’s below. You’ll still have your Missile.”

Across the hall the bowman’s string finally caught the notch. He tested the pull, and reached for the quiver at his hip. Jakal rose, spoke the incantation, and scattered sand and rose petals toward the brazier. The magic took hold at once. The bowman folded where he stood, and the two dicers sagged over their barrel as though someone had cut their strings. Nobody had to draw a blade. They tied the sleepers with cord, stripped them of daggers, bow and coin, and went on.

In a webbed chamber to the north a giant spider hung in the ceiling silk. Jakal raised his hands, spoke the words of power, and sent a dart of blue-white force streaking over Calmert’s shoulder. It struck the creature squarely between its clustered eyes with a crack like breaking slate, and tore it down onto the flagstones. “Clean strike, scholar,” Elmo muttered, resting his axe head against his knee. Jakal felt the last of his arcane strength go out of him. The two spells he had been granted for the day were spent, and it was not yet noon.

He searched a cocoon and found a dead man in it, and a purse: a dozen gold pieces, some silver, and an unlabelled clay vial that sloshed. His herb training told him what the smell meant. “The sharp bitterwort and snakeroot aroma,” he wrote in his own memory, “confirmed it is an herbal antidote against poison.” Elmo, who had seen the spider’s work, approved. “That’ll keep a man breathing if another one of those creeping crawlers bites.” Calmert said a word to St. Cuthbert for the dead man in the silk.

· · ·

Then they went down. The cellar corridor was wet and quiet. A barred door on the west side was left alone; Elmo whispered that nothing was moving behind it but that it stank of the grave. In a pillared storeroom, three dog-sized rats launched from the mouldering sacks, and Jakal, retreating half a pace into the shadow of a pillar, gave his first orders in a fight.

Darts and daggers, Spug — hit ’em! We hang back to give the fighter and cleric room.

Calmert caught the first on his shield and split its skull with a downward blow of his mace. Elmo’s handaxe cleaved the second in mid-leap. Jakal snapped his wrist and an iron dart went through the third rat’s flank, and Spugnoir’s thrown knife pinned its neck to the stone. Afterward, when it was quiet, a rat’s bite on his thumb began to throb. He bound it with a strip of his robe hem, and on the plinth of a pillar he beat a bent dart straight. “This is the only dart I have left,” he said. “Your yelp carried,” said Elmo, “Quick about it.”

The gnolls came at a choke point in a stone passage, with a torch wedged behind the company. A dog-muzzled brute with a spear barked once and fled howling, and something deeper in the dark howled back. Jakal set Calmert at the bend, Elmo behind his shoulder, Spugnoir beside himself, and reached for the Magic Missile he had spent on a spider.

It was gone. His account is plain about this: “I drew my last straightened iron dart from my belt and took aim past Calmert’s shoulder.” It took the axe-gnoll in the throat. When a second gnoll thrust its spear past the scrum, “I deflected the iron point with my staff into the stone wall.” Pinned against Calmert’s shield, a third snarled, and Jakal, fighting hand to hand for the first time against gnolls, “leaned over Calmert’s shoulder and drove my belt dagger into the pinned gnoll’s neck.” The last one cut Calmert’s shin and fled.

“It’s gone to fetch the rest,” Elmo said.

Let’s retreat quickly. Back to where we left the brigands tied up. Back to the hall — move!

He healed Calmert’s shin on the way, woke the bound brigands, and marched them up into the light and down to Hommlet for justice. It was a respectable first descent: three sleeping brigands and a cocoon’s purse, bought with two spells and a bent dart. But the keep’s captain was not impressed. Rufus, “deeming our intelligence incomplete due to our hasty retreat,” paid a reduced bounty of fifteen gold instead of twenty and cautioned that the moathouse remained dangerous. Jakal saw the strain in the company on the way back down to the village, and set it down in his own account: “My magic had been exhausted in the cellars, leaving us vulnerable when the gnolls pressed in.” It was the first lesson the moathouse had for him. He would be taught it again.

· · ·

On the village green, under the weeping willow, a road-dusty sellsword with a round shield and an arming sword crossed the grass to the company and offered his blade. His name was Dunstan Mere. Jakal had one thing to give him, and gave it plainly.

We will split all the treasure equally. That is all we can offer, sir. Is that acceptable?

Dunstan took the terms, and the company under the willow said again what it had already agreed. Elmo took a grass stem out of his mouth: “Equal shares was my price from the start, and I’ll hold to it.” Calmert laid a hand on his shield. “The church asks no more than its share. Equal it is.” Spugnoir smiled. “It was agreed before. It’s agreed again.” For the first time since the cellars, the narrator wrote, they seemed easy together. The company was five.

There was one more small thing before noon, and it is worth keeping. Jakal had it fixed in his head that Spugnoir had lost the vial of holy water. He said so, with some firmness, and proposed that they go and look for it in their room at the Wench. Spugnoir, who was a patient man, opened his satchel and took the vial out. It had been there all morning. Ostler, meanwhile, had let their upper chamber to the Wild Coast sellsword Zert and charged them fresh silver for a cramped back room over the kitchen, and that is where the next part of the story begins.

Part ThreeThe Seven Sisters

World days 3–5 · played 27–28 September

The back room over the kitchen was hot and it stank of grease. The shutter had swollen shut. The bolt on the door hung from a single screw. Jakal, who had already come back to find his first room let to someone else, jammed an iron spike under the warped door to wedge it fast, and balanced a tin cup and a loose nail on the latch for an alarm. Then he sat down at the table, took out his last scarred dart, and turned it over in his fingers, thinking of the one that had gone into a gnoll’s throat.

It was a scarred, bent thing. He suspected there was something in it.

I need to recover that dart I stuck in the gnoll’s throat. It’s a special dart, one I suspect has latent magic in it that just needs to be awakened. What do you think, Spug?

Spugnoir set down his quill and said what a friend would say. “A dart forged of common iron does not grow an enchantment simply by finding a throat, Jakal.”

The noon heat pressed through the floor. Downstairs pans clattered. And Jakal, who had been taught what every apprentice is taught, said the thing that the rest of this story leans on.

Yes, that is what we have been taught, isn’t it, Spug. Perhaps our teachings are the limits of current understanding. There is magic in the world yet undiscovered. You and I will find and bring to life the magic hidden in the world. This I swear to you by the seven sisters.

Marc, as Jakal, 27 September

Spugnoir was quiet for a moment. Then he smiled, a little embarrassed, reached across the grease-stained table and gripped Jakal’s wrist.

“I don’t know your seven sisters, Jakal, but I’ll hold you to the oath all the same. We find it together, and we write it down properly. And we do it alive, not over scrap iron.”

Spugnoir of Dyvers

He let go and went back to his notes, still smiling. The record never says what the seven sisters are. It does say that the oath was made once, in a hot room, between two young men with little magic to speak of, and that twenty-two days later by the world’s count, in a library in Verbobonc, one of the first questions Jakal put to a teacher was how new spells are made.

The afternoon went to the matter at hand. “Perhaps there is another way into their lair,” Jakal said. “I agree the frontal assault would end badly for us all.” Spugnoir had been thinking along the same lines. Water had been trickling in that cellar corridor. “Water that comes into a cellar has to leave it somewhere,” he said, “and in a moathouse sunk in the fens that means a drain, or a culvert out to the marsh. And Burne said the Temple folk built the place. Men like that never dug a cellar without a bolt-hole.” Elmo knew those fens better than anyone.

· · ·

Dunstan was late to the east road at dawn. Elmo was missing altogether, until he stepped out of a hawthorn hedge, explaining that he had been scouting it to be sure nobody from the taproom had followed them. Jakal greeted the sellsword with relief: “We were worried those ruffians had waylaid you.” He told the company that valiant friends were journeying toward Hommlet and would reach it by evening, and Elmo welcomed the thought. “More steel will make short work of those dog-faced brutes in the cellars,” he said. “We can scout the fen for the old drain culvert today, mark the way, and fall back to the taproom to meet your folk before nightfall.” Spugnoir agreed that mapping the runoff now would prepare them for a decisive assault once their allies came. So the plan for the day was a small one. They would look for the culvert, and go home.

On the march east Dunstan told Jakal that he would take the front beside Elmo at the moathouse and keep the fire and oil behind him. Jakal searched the ditches and hedge-banks as they walked, and cut comfrey and yarrow for wounds, a fistful of feverfew, and under a hawthorn root a rare clump of silver-leaved woundwort, which his herbal masters valued above the rest for closing cuts. Where the cart track dissolved into fenland mire he crushed the woundwort between two flat stones into a fragrant paste and folded it into a clean linen poultice. Dunstan asked Elmo about sentries, and Elmo warned them of lookouts on the high stone and of marsh beasts along the causeway.

At the moathouse the upper parapets looked empty. Dunstan drew his blade, saw giant frogs wallowing sluggishly in the scummy water beside the causeway, and warned the company to keep close. Jakal stayed behind the front rank with his staff, with Calmert and Spugnoir beside him, watching the moat for any stirring. Nothing stirred. But at the gate arch Dunstan accidentally kicked loose a piece of mortar, and froze against the stone, and from inside the moathouse came a distant scrape of iron and a muffled snarl. He raised his fist to halt the file and whispered back a warning. Jakal readied his staff and signalled to Spugnoir, and the wood clacked against a loose flag underfoot. Ahead, Dunstan peered around the arch. Across the weeds, in the shadow of the great hall doorway, a gnoll sentry stood with a poleaxe.

Jakal whispered to Elmo, asking whether he could take out the gnome quietly. Elmo corrected him: it was a gnoll, and he could, if it kept its snout up. His arrow took the creature under the jaw and it dropped without a sound. But its poleaxe clanged on the step, and something in the dark hall growled and began to stir.

Jakal warned the company that something was coming and called for everyone to take positions and wait for it. Dunstan stepped to the arch with an oil flask ready, and Calmert braced with his shield. Across the courtyard two gnolls ducked behind the hall pillars, and an arrow zipped across the weeds to splinter on the gate stone beside Dunstan. Jakal stepped to the gate opening and wove his Sleep across the yard toward the hall doorway. It took the gnoll behind the left pillar and dropped it into the briars; the second beast resisted the fringe of the spell and cried out an alarm. Dunstan warned of the killing ground of open yard. Elmo told of a briar-choked breach in the east wall. From deep inside the ruins came the sound of running feet.

Two more gnolls with spears charged out of the hall. Dunstan smashed a flask of lamp oil across their path, ten paces out on the flagstones, and roared for the wizard to bring fire. Jakal had an iron dart in his hand, and loosed it past Dunstan’s shoulder into the lead beast’s chest, which checked their rush at the slick. Then he struck flint and steel in frantic haste at Dunstan’s shout. “The cold sparks fizzled on the damp causeway flags without catching tinder or reaching the oil puddle ten paces out.” A thrown javelin splintered against Dunstan’s shield, and an arrow rang off the entry stone, and the gnolls began to skirt the slick.

A third gnoll came round the flank. Jakal reached for another dart and found his belt loops empty, so he brought up his staff behind Dunstan. The gnoll’s spear scored Dunstan’s thigh as he cut the beast above the knee and dropped it in the archway, and Jakal cracked his staff across its muzzle. It dragged itself back into the weeds on its bad leg. When the next arrow from the hall splintered on the jamb, Jakal used the arch for cover, stepped out, brought his quarterstaff down hard on the lamed gnoll in the briars, and laid it dead, then pulled back behind the stone before the archer could draw a bead. Dunstan knelt to bind his wounded leg and told them to hold the choke while the last spearman retreated. Crouched behind the masonry, Jakal pressed the silver-leaved woundwort poultice under the bandage, and it knit cleanly against the spear cut and halted the blood.

The sleeping gnoll by the pillar began to wake and crawl. Dunstan braced his shield in the opening to cover Elmo, and an arrow slammed into its rim. Jakal cast his Magic Missile at the bowman across the yard, a bolt of force that struck it dead in the chest. But the crawling gnoll slipped into the hall despite Dunstan’s shout and Elmo’s shot, which glanced off the stone, and from the dark interior came harsh barking and the pounding of boots.

Jakal asked the others what the plan was. Dunstan had one. Calmert would light a brand now, and when the gnolls reached the spilled oil he would throw his second flask on top of it, and the brand would go in after. Calmert got a brush torch burning. Elmo said he would shoot the one giving orders. Spugnoir said he would hold the arch against anything that got through the fire. Shapes were massing in the hall doorway, and something big was growling.

Jakal warned Spugnoir to ready his flames for the gnoll leader barking from the rear. Dunstan shattered the second flask into the charging brutes, and Calmert’s torch turned the puddle into a roaring conflagration. Two gnolls were caught in the blaze. Beyond it a massive beast in thick hide tried to rally the survivors. Jakal told Spugnoir to hold his Burning Hands ready in case the chieftain charged through the wall of smoke; he never had to. Dunstan called out the leader’s position to Elmo and cut down a burning beast that staggered through the gate arch, and Elmo’s arrow struck the chieftain in the shoulder and checked the rally behind the blazing oil.

Then the fire died to embers across the stones, and the wounded chieftain vanished into the dark doorway of the great hall. What happened next is told two ways. Jakal’s own memory has him calling on the company to advance and finish the gnolls now, and also telling Dunstan that they were safe holding the gatehouse; Spugnoir remembers Dunstan counselling the gate and Jakal pressing the pursuit. Either way, Dunstan called back that he was going first, and crossed the smoking courtyard alone. As he reached the great hall’s threshold a javelin flew from the darkness inside and struck him hard. It split the rim of his shield and gouged his collarbone. Jakal stepped to the arch and hurled a dart across the courtyard at the doorway to cover his retreat. It bit into the doorpost and drove the gnoll back into the shadows, and Elmo sent an arrow after it to keep the entrance pinned while Calmert held his shield ready, and Dunstan pulled back across the smoking cinders.

Jakal hauled him behind Calmert’s shield at the arch and looked at the torn shoulder. The javelin had gouged a deep furrow in the flesh, but the bone was whole. He cleaned the wound and bound it tightly with herbal salve to staunch the bleeding. Across the courtyard the gnolls stayed back in the shadows of the great hall and would not venture into the open daylight. Then he laid his hand on the shoulder and spoke the words of the rite.

I laid my hand over Dunstan’s shoulder and spoke the words of the restorative rite. Warmth flowed from my fingers into the wound, mending the torn sinew beneath the linen wrapping.

From Jakal’s own memory

Elmo kept his bow trained on the hall’s dark entrance while Calmert held the archway with his shield. When the rite was done they conferred behind the gatehouse wall. Elmo advised against forcing the barricaded hall doors, since the gnolls held every advantage across the open courtyard, and urged them to circle south through the reeds to look for the drain instead. Calmert and Spugnoir agreed that slipping through the outflow would bypass the gnolls’ fortified choke point.

That was the day’s healing, and Jakal had given it away. They went round through the tall reeds along the southern moat, watching the parapets, until in the shadow of the south wall Elmo parted a curtain of thorns and showed them a low stone culvert trickling foul water into the marsh: the outflow that Spugnoir and Jakal had guessed at. Dunstan cut the briars away with his sword and found a clear, narrow drain, three feet high. Jakal urged them, all the same, to hold off. Dunstan had no shield, his own darts were nearly gone, and his friends were arriving that night. Dunstan agreed, and as he dragged the cut briars back over the mouth of the drain the dry thorns crackled, and a gnoll leaned over the south wall, looked down at the cut brush and at them, and ducked away; barking went up inside. Elmo agreed they should go, but warned that they would be minding the drain when they came back.

At the smithy Dunstan haggled an oak shield from ten gold to eight, and Jakal bought two more throwing knives at two gold apiece. The third, said Smyth, had a loose tang and would be mended by next week.

· · ·

Jakal was an honest student, and the next hour taught him an honest lesson. In the heat of that kitchen room, with smoke from the hearth curling between the boards, he found himself asking a question. He was part cleric. Why shouldn’t he carry a mace and wear mail, like Calmert? His old masters’ voices gave him the answer. A priest’s blunt iron is no sin, and prayers rise as well through chain as through cloth. But the Art is different. He raised his arms stiffly, as though weighted, and began the passes of Sleep, and the pattern went sluggish and wrong. Across the table Spugnoir looked up from his notes with watering eyes.

“Wear iron and you can say goodbye to half your Art. Keep the half that prays and lose the half that burns.”

Spugnoir of Dyvers

It is worth holding onto that line.

The village was empty in the heat. Jakal had gone out for air and was walking south along the dusty cart track when he heard heavy footsteps close fast behind him, and before he could swing his staff round a massive hand clenched in his collar and hurled him into the ditch. It is not much of an ending, and it is not one. Jakal has been in Hommlet for thirty days by the world’s own count. He arrived with a staff, a dagger and a head full of what he had been taught, and in that time he has learned to put a company to sleep with a handful of sand, to carry a mace, to hand the call to the woodsman who should have it, and to sit with a widow on a porch step. He has been thrown into a ditch and put out of a church, lost a staff and, for a few minutes, the use of an ankle. He has sworn an oath to a friend to find magic that does not yet exist, and a druid has told him, and a canon through Calmert, that he cannot be both priest and mage. He has dedicated both of his callings to a god who does not mind. And he keeps checking the post.

What is still open

  • The altar. It was quieted, not cleansed. Jaroo says hammers must break it and the fen be watched, and Canon Terjon promised hammers before nightfall. The record does not say whether they were ever swung.
  • Davl and Gremag. Whistling Pete named them to Rufus, and Corporal Ersn and four men were sent to secure the trading post, yet on the morning of the twenty-second the traders are behind their counter, unmolested, selling the hedge-priest his answer.
  • Turuko, Kobort and Zert. Still watching, still at their trestle, still, as far as Jakal can tell, spies for whatever lies under the moathouse.
  • The silver mace. Wrapped in linen, tainted with the cruelty of its maker, kept on purpose. Such steel has a way of remembering who commands it.
  • The grimoire. Six dweomers, two of them loose between the gatherings, and the book wanting proper bookbinding paste.
  • The oath. Twenty-two days old when Eldred called its first question madness. Neither Jakal nor Spugnoir has found a new spell yet.

How this was made

The story was built from three things the world kept. First, Jakal’s own memories in the Greyhawk database: 804 of them, from the first evening to the morning of 22 October. Second, the narrator’s answers to Jakal, and the exact words Marc typed for him, which the prompt log keeps pulse by pulse. Third, what other people in the world said to Jakal, as the narrator told it to him. I did not use anyone else’s private memory, so nothing here comes from inside another character’s head.

Marc’s typed lines are printed with capitals and spelling mended and otherwise as he wrote them; where the narrator rendered an action as speech, that rendering is used. Other characters’ words in quotation marks are exactly the narrator’s. The prose between them is mine, and it follows the record: the order of events comes from the tick order, the dates from the witness logs (UTC), and where the record contradicts itself (who cast Sleep in the willow hollow; what the dart count was; whether the clay vial shattered; the dead farmer’s sons’ names; whether Dunstan walked up to the company on the green or nobody was there; whether Spugnoir or Jakal was carrying the holy water; whether Dunstan was in the Wench on the evening of the sixteenth; where Elmo was at the dawn muster) I took the version Jakal himself saw, or left the point out. A few garbled typed clauses are straightened, and the world’s clock, which turns on its players’ turns, runs differently from the log’s wall-clock dates, so where this telling says “that night” or “the next morning” it follows the narrator’s clock.

Where the record is silent, this telling is silent: why Jakal hid the old staff in the oak; why he went to Verbobonc; what the seven sisters are; who the friends he waits for may be; what became of the altar. Those are Marc’s to answer, or the world’s.
