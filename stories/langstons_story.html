The Tale of Daniel Langston

One Saturday in Arkham, a hinge in a cellar, and a lake under two suns · 28 April 1928

“I am not leading them toward the Great Ones. I am conducting an experiment in which all of us—including myself—are variables.”

Daniel Langston

The Man in the Chalk Dust

He came up from the Arkham streets, a short, heavyset man with an untrimmed red beard and chalk-dusted clothes, his pockets stuffed with papers, and went into a large stone mansion whose windows were mostly shuttered. Its walls were dark and weathered. Chalk dust and scraps of paper lay about its rooms, and from the upper windows the steeples of Miskatonic University stood over the gambrel roofs of the town.

Inside, the floor was littered with chalk dust and old papers, awaiting the proper notation. His eyes traced the subtler lines where the corners of the room failed to meet at proper right angles. Somewhere beneath the campus Edwin Pike tended the unmapped corridors, and in his locked drawer Notebook Four kept its silent count. He adjusted his waistcoat, turned his hand through the dim air to test the local cross-section, and listened for whatever boundary was beginning to shift.

His name was Daniel Langston. He was a professor of physics at Miskatonic University, short, heavyset, balding, and possessed of an enormous red beard that had grown progressively less disciplined as his research advanced. His clothes were expensive and badly kept. His waistcoats were frequently dusted with chalk and his cuffs stained with ink, and his pockets held scraps of paper covered in equations he could not remember writing. He was eccentric and brilliant, an accomplished mathematician, and one of the world’s foremost, if largely unrecognized, students of the deep structure of reality.

He spoke in geometric terms. People approached one another along intersecting trajectories. Disagreements were incompatible coordinate systems, secrets were occluded dimensions, and death was a change of embedding. His lectures were notorious for beginning with perfectly respectable tensor mathematics and ending somewhere deeply uncomfortable. He was socially inept, absent-minded, vain about his mathematics, and almost completely incapable of recognizing when another person had become frightened of him.

Secretly, he was the head of the Cult of Everlasting Light. There were nine of them, himself included, and the other eight were devoted to establishing stable bridges between our reality and the greater manifold beyond it. He did not worship Yog-Sothoth. One does not worship a manifold. One learns its curvature.

Two of the eight were useful to him. Dr. Miriam Vale was a former Miskatonic neurologist who now studied the geometry of memory. She was quiet, severe, and almost impossible to surprise. Edwin Pike was a university groundskeeper with a talent for finding doors where no doors should exist, and he claimed that the campus had begun growing new corridors beneath the older buildings. Langston had confirmed this.

He had learned how to summon a byakhee. The first successful experiment had destroyed fourteen windows in the physics building, inverted every compass within approximately seventy yards, caused Professor Armitage’s pocket watch to run backward for three hours, and left a graduate student unable to perceive right angles until the following Tuesday. In his opinion it had been an outstanding success.

Beneath an abandoned observatory annex he kept a private laboratory, and at its center stood a complicated brass instrument of nested rings. It was not an astronomical instrument. It was a hinge. He called it the Langston Rotator, and his colleagues believed the name an affectionate nickname for a mathematical apparatus. They were wrong.

In locked cabinets he kept notebooks, and the fourth was the one he did not remember writing. It was in his handwriting. Several of its pages referred to experiments he had not yet performed. One page described his death, and the date had been crossed out twice. He found this encouraging.

And there was the dream. A city stood beside a black lake beneath unfamiliar stars. Sometimes he stood on its shore, sometimes above it, and sometimes he was the lake. There were two suns, although one might actually be an opening. Someone waited for him there, wearing a pale mask. They had never spoken.

The Cult considered him its master, which was a misunderstanding. He was not leading them toward the Great Ones. He was conducting an experiment in which all of them, himself included, were variables. And if he was correct, the barrier separating mankind from the greater realities was not a wall at all. It was merely an angle, and angles can be changed.

Eleven O’Clock

At twelve minutes before ten on a brisk Saturday morning, the twenty-eighth of April, 1928, he drew a silver pocket watch from his chalk-dusted waistcoat. Ahh. When was his next lecture, in this primitive geometry?

He thumbed through the rumpled university schedule tucked into his breast pocket. It demanded his presence in the lecture hall at eleven o’clock, for the second-year discourse on Newtonian mechanics and elementary kinematics: a tedious recitation of Euclidean three-space and flat lines, but necessary to preserve his academic standing in Arkham. That left him over an hour in the quiet of the mansion.

He gave the hour to his notes. He scribbled brilliant insights into the shearing forces that occur when a hyper-geometric reality is occluded by a lower geometric projection, and his pen scratched out the equations for shear forces where higher-dimensional topologies intersect three-dimensional manifolds, a crude shadow play that this world mistook for immutable law. When his watch showed eleven nearly upon him he shoved the ink-wet foolscap and the chalk stubs into his coat and hurried down the hill toward the university steeples. He was running late, and his students were idiots. Perhaps there would be one among them he could spark something in.

The spring mist had cleared from the campus paths. He strode into the physics lecture hall just as the second-year students took their seats.

“Forget the textbook’s assumptions of static space,” he announced, turning to the blackboard with chalk in hand. As he outlined the vectors of elementary mechanics, his gaze caught a pale young man in the front row, following each coordinate shift with unsettling, silent comprehension. To his surprise, at least one student in the front row followed the non-Euclidean vector adjustments without the usual dull blankness.

It was 10:54 when he began in earnest, with a vector problem that hinted at higher-dimensional curvature. He turned to the slate with chalk in hand, sketching a three-dimensional Cartesian frame before warping the axes with an additional metric tensor, indicating where an apparent divergence resolved into a closed curve in four-space. He looked to see whether any of them faintly comprehended, and he laid down the chalk and turned back to the tiered benches to pause for questions. The room was quiet except for the scratching of lead pencils and the dull rumble of a radiator pipe. The student in the front row, the one who had seemed attentive a moment ago, blinked slowly at the board, then lifted a hesitant hand.

“Sir, isn’t the curl of that field zero in the textbook? Is this required for the midterm?”

A few students glanced up from their notebooks, waiting only to see whether they needed to copy the strange symbols down into their margins. None of them saw the curve at all.

He dismissed the question. “The syllabus is a three-dimensional fiction for clerks,” he snapped, grinding the chalk against the slate until it shrieked and splintered. He turned his back on the boy and began dragging the broken nub across the blackboard in frantic, jagged arcs. He did not merely adjust the vectors; he forced the higher tensor coordinates into the flat surface, scribbling the forbidden angles of spatial shearing where mass distribution curves inward upon itself. The equations twisted and bunched into grotesque, impossible projections that seemed to slant unnaturally in the classroom light, defying perspective. A sharp wave of vertigo prickled behind his eyes as the chalk dust thickened around his fingers.

When he paused, breathing heavily, with gray dust clinging to his beard and lapels, the front row was dead quiet. The student had drawn back in his seat, staring not at the math but at him, his notebook snapped shut in alarm. When he turned back, covered in white dust, the undergraduates sat in rigid, frightened silence.

“Copy it,” he said, pointing the chalk-stained stub toward the warped gridwork of equations spanning the slate. “Every index, every transformation coefficient. In three minutes the board will be cleared, and whatever you fail to transcribe will remain beyond your grasp.”

A frantic rustle of notebook paper and scratching pens answered him. In the front row the nervous student fumbled his pen, ink spattering across his margins as he tried to duplicate the unnatural, skewed angles, his gaze darting between the slate and his page with wide, uncomprehending dread. Behind him the rest of the hall bent in anxious haste over their desks. None of these clerks possessed the faculty to understand the geometries, but their fear ensured they committed the higher coordinates to ink.

He seized the felt eraser from the trough and swept it across the slate, obliterating the skewed vectors and tensor indices in broad, chalky smears. A cloud of white dust billowed out, settling thick upon his sleeves and drifting over the front desk. He tossed the eraser down with a sharp clatter and turned his back to the board.

“That will be all. Leave.”

The undergraduates did not wait for a second dismissal. Notebooks snapped shut, pens were capped with frantic, ink-stained fingers, and chairs scraped loudly against the floorboards as the room scrambled for the double doors. The student in the front row scooped his blotched notes into his leather folder and bolted with the rest, eyes cast down to avoid his gaze. Within seconds the hurried footsteps faded down the corridor, leaving him alone in the high, silent hall amidst the falling dust.

It was six minutes past eleven.

A Hinge Under the Annex

His expectations, it appeared, had been too high. Whatever. He would seek out his two cultists and describe his latest findings in detail.

He left the chalk-hazed lecture hall behind and crossed the bright quadrangle toward the neglected fringe of the campus, where the old observatory annex stood half-smothered in ivy. He slipped past the padlocked exterior grating and went down the narrow brick stair into the vault beneath, and there, already at work beside the massive brass armature of the Langston Rotator, he found Dr. Miriam Vale and the groundskeeper Edwin Pike. The cool subterranean air carried the faint, dry scent of ozone and old dust. Vale looked up from her ink-ruled ledger while Pike leaned his heavy iron bar against the masonry wall.

He spread his crumpled sheets of tensor equations across the workbench and traced the higher-dimensional shear forces with a dusty fingernail. “The shear coefficients confirm our rotation along the eighth coordinate,” he said, and laid out how the spatial anomalies would distort the local geometry once the brass rings turned. Vale nodded, recording the indices without hesitation. By the time he had finished it was 11:22.

He ordered Pike and Dr. Vale to calibrate the Rotator’s nested rings to the eighth coordinate, using the new shear indices. “Set the third gimbal to the seventh shear index,” he told them, pointing chalk-stained fingers toward the nested brass rings. Vale opened her ledger flat across the timber trestle, calling out the calculated coordinate offsets in a crisp, measured tone. Pike hauled his heavy iron spanner against the bevel gears, his boots scraping the flagstones as he levered the outer ring into alignment. The heavy brass groaned against its mountings. As the inner hoop reached the prescribed angular shear, metal screeched violently on metal, and the primary gimbal bound against its housing, resisting the higher tensor tilt. Pike threw his full weight into the iron lever, teeth gritted, forcing the gear past the burr with a shudder that jarred the stone beneath Langston’s feet. The rings settled into the calculated posture, but the housing hummed with a strained, unnatural vibration that refused to dissipate. The physical metal strained under the dimensional torsion, exactly as the equations predicted.

At 11:28 he threw the iron switch of the primary drive. Heavy brass gearing bit with a deafening shriek, setting the nested rings of the Langston Rotator into blurring gyroscopic motion. The high hum deepened into a gut-churning thrum that vibrated through the bedrock. Before his eyes the vault’s right angles began to fold upon themselves. Shadows stretched along unnatural diagonals, and a sickening, non-Euclidean curvature bent the light into nauseating parabolas. An icy, mineral-scented draught poured from nowhere as the eighth coordinate began to open. But the physical metal groaned under the dimensional torsion. A sharp crack echoed through the chamber as the flags beneath the northern footing splintered under unnatural shear. Pike leaned his full weight against the juddering retaining bar to keep the assembly from tearing loose, while Vale rapidly charted the screaming harmonics in her ledger. The threshold wavered, violent and unstable, straining against its brass harness.

“Apply the third harmonic correction from the ledger now!” he shouted over the screech of spinning metal.

Pike stepped into the unnatural downdraft, his coat whipping against his knees, and turned the fine brass vernier on the side bracket. He seized the weighted throttle bar and eased back the steam flow by half an inch. The jarring vibration that had threatened to rip the stone anchors from the floor softened into a steady, predatory purr. Between the counter-rotating rings the grey air did not merely blur; it parted. The vault sagged under skewed perspectives, corners stretching into obtuse voids, but the aperture held firm. Through the brass perimeter, the laboratory brickwork vanished into a dim expanse of black water and impossible shorelines beneath a dead sky.

Black Water, Two Suns

At 11:32 Langston stepped forward to the rim of the spinning brass gimbals, and the air dropped sharply to a bitter, stagnant cold. Through the aperture the damp cellar wall had vanished entirely. In its place lay a boundless expanse of black, glass-smooth water beneath an ink-dark sky pierced by two pale, sickly suns. Along the distant curvature of the shore rose the shattered silhouettes of unearthly spires, basalt towers whose angles mocked every Euclidean axiom he had ever taught. The view was impossibly sharp, steady in its higher coordinate alignment.

His memory would give the place its name: a stark vista of Carcosa, silent black water stretching beneath twin pale suns, bordered by distant spires of non-Euclidean geometry. The higher-dimensional alignment held with remarkable stability, confirming his tensor calculations down to the lowest shear coefficient.

Behind him Dr. Vale stepped up with her ledger raised to confirm the coordinate planes, but as her gaze locked with the unnatural horizon, she stiffened. The fountain pen dropped from her numb fingers, splattering black across the flags. A dark line of blood crept from her nostril as she stared ahead in glassy, catatonic silence, heedless of Pike bracing the shuddering clutch bar.

He shut down the Langston.

He seized the primary throttle and threw the heavy clutch lever into reverse. The Rotator screeched in mechanical protest as the friction blocks bit into the brass rim. A ferocious shudder ripped through the iron frame, and the sudden deceleration kicked the brake lever violently from Pike’s grip, throwing him back against the cellar wall with a bruised wrist and a sharp curse. Between the spinning rings the black waters and pale twin suns of Carcosa rippled, distorted, and folded violently in upon themselves. With a flat, hollow snap of displaced air, the aperture vanished. The unnatural frost collapsed into the musty warmth of subterranean Arkham, leaving only the smell of hot grease, scorched metal, and ozone. On the stone floor Dr. Vale drew a ragged, shuddering breath as the paralysis broke, wiping the dark smear of blood from her upper lip.

At 11:35 he stepped past the smoking drive shaft to where she knelt upon the damp flags. Her breathing was ragged against the lingering reek of scorched oil and ozone. When he lifted the open ledger from the floor beside her dropped pen, he found that the black ink had pooled across the paper, blotting the final lines of the harmonic offset series into an unreadable smear. Vale leaned back against the rough masonry of the footing, her eyes slowly clearing from the glazed stupor, though she pressed two fingers to her temple as if still fighting an unvoiced rhythm beneath her thoughts. Across the vault Pike cradled his swollen wrist against his apron and watched the cooling gimbals settle into place.

The Afternoon of the Fourth Notebook

It had been an amazing success. The triumph of the aperture’s opening still burned behind his eyes. He turned from the silent Rotator and looked down at Dr. Vale as she wiped the dark stain from her chin, and at Pike where he leaned against the damp masonry.

“We’ll meet in the basement of my mansion as we do regularly,” he told them, his voice cutting through the ringing quiet of the vault. “Clean this up and inspect the machinery. That was quite a strain. Meet me there tonight.”

Vale got to her feet, pressing a handkerchief to her nose, and nodded in quiet agreement. She promised to convey the summons to the remaining six members of the Cult of Everlasting Light before dusk. Pike grunted, wrapping a greasy rag around his swollen wrist before kneeling to inspect the sheared anchor bolts along the floor. The sight of Carcosa’s twin suns had proved the absolute validity of Langston’s non-Euclidean tensor formulations, and he left them to secure the vault.

It was 11:40. He climbed the stone steps from the vault into the quiet annex and emerged into the midday light. He crossed the campus grounds and ascended the hill through Arkham toward his shuttered stone mansion, the April wind biting at his coat. Inside the heavy oak doors the house was cold and silent, smelling of dry chalk and old binding glue. He descended to the spacious cellar, cleared the central flagstones, and arranged the perimeter benches for the evening’s gathering of nine. Moving to the locked oak cabinet in the corner, he turned the brass key and took out the four worn notebooks, laying them across the trestle. The familiar ink traces of his own handwriting stared back from the final volume: pages recording dates yet unlived, and the singular line where his death was twice crossed out.

It was five past noon.

He worked his equations, trying to identify the geometric location of lost Carcosa. He spent the long afternoon bent over the fourth notebook, filling sheet after sheet of foolscap with tensor proofs to bracket the higher-dimensional coordinates of the black lake. Without the terminal offsets that Vale’s ink had taken, the equations refused to close entirely, but the spatial azimuth held within a narrow, reproducible margin.

When the pale April daylight began to fail against the high mansion windows, he went down to the cellar again. He trimmed the copper-arc lamps until their sputtering glare cast unnatural, violet shadows against the damp foundation. He judged that the strange shifting lighting effects he had created in the basement added the correct atmosphere, and that the black basalt benches in a circle were a nice touch. He tested the heavy wiring along the masonry and arranged the stone seats in their wide ring around the central flagstones. The electrically charged traps were good for electrocuting any overly inquisitive outsider. The sonic traps were probably overkill, and would definitely disable an intruder. He had to remember to deactivate and set them properly before and after each meeting. Every precaution against intrusion was primed, and then switched off at the junction box for the evening’s arrival.

Then he went upstairs to the front parlor to wait for his cult members. It was twenty-five past six.

The Three-Beat Knock

He sat in the shadowed front parlor while the evening fog thickened against the mansion’s tall, shuttered windows. The street outside fell into a heavy damp stillness, the distant university bells chiming the quarters into the gathering dark. At ten minutes past seven the heavy iron knocker struck the front door in the measured three-beat cadence of the circle. He drew back the heavy bolts and admitted Dr. Miriam Vale and Edwin Pike, who stepped across the threshold into the hall followed closely by the remaining brethren from across Arkham. Vale appeared entirely recovered, her coat buttoned against the river chill, and Pike secured the latch behind them without a sound. Not a soul had followed them through the quiet lanes. The brethren assembled in hushed anticipation, ready to descend to the basement and examine the coordinates of the higher reality he had breached.

At a quarter past seven they all went downstairs.

He led Vale, Pike, and the rest of the circle down the narrow stone stairs into the cellar. Under the shifting glare of the arc lamps the brethren took their places along the curved basalt benches. He described to the assembled his breakthroughs of the day and the great success of the Langston. He laid out the morning’s triumph in the annex: how the non-Euclidean shear tensors had forced the Rotator’s rings into alignment, opening a stable rift directly onto the black waters of Carcosa beneath its twin pale suns. He scoffed at the dullards in his morning mechanics class who could never grasp such higher geometry.

Yet when he attempted to demonstrate the coordinate proofs, the ruined harmonic offsets from Vale’s ledger left the equations incomplete. Vale stiffened in sudden, glassy silence, while Pike spoke up about the damaged apparatus.

“The frame held, but the south footing is cracked through,” Pike warned.

In his memory Vale had fallen into an unnerving catatonic daze in the middle of the discussion. The cult accepted the reality of Carcosa, though the missing offsets and the mechanical damage left them uneasy.

At 7:40 he stepped into the center of the basalt circle and raised his hands to cut through the anxious murmurs. He explained that structural shearing and physiological vertigo were merely predictable boundary phenomena when lower-dimensional projections met higher topologies. He directed Pike to secure heavy channel iron and threaded tie-rods to brace the cracked south footing before the apparatus was turned again. For the lost harmonic offsets, he instructed Vale to bring her surviving antecedent scratch-sheets to his study tomorrow, so that they might jointly re-derive the obliterated coefficients through inverse tensor transformation. Pike nodded in grim comprehension, calculating the ironwork needed, while Vale pressed a handkerchief to her temple and agreed to produce her preliminary drafts. The brethren settled back into their seats, their misgivings quieted by the methodical division of labor, though no one looked entirely at ease under the flickering cellar lamps.

With the path to their next alignment clearly defined, he prepared to conclude the convocation. It was twelve minutes before eight.

Who Is Who

Daniel Langston is the only player in this story, and he is played by a person. Everyone else belongs to the world.

Daniel Langston. Professor of physics at Miskatonic University and, secretly, the head of the Cult of Everlasting Light. Short, heavyset, balding, with an enormous red beard. Played by Marc, who wrote the account of him and played all nineteen of his turns.

Dr. Miriam Vale. A former Miskatonic neurologist who studies the geometry of memory, and keeper of the coordinate ledger. The world’s own character, not a player’s.

Edwin Pike. A university groundskeeper with a talent for finding doors, who works the Rotator’s spanner and iron bar. The world’s own character, not a player’s.

The student in the front row. A pale young man, unnamed, who asked whether it would be on the midterm.

The brethren. The remaining six members of the Cult of Everlasting Light. Unnamed; the record prepares for six and does not say how many came.

Professor Armitage and the Dean. Never present. They figure only in the account of Langston’s past: a pocket watch that ran backward, the back of a coat covered in diagrams.

The Narrator. The voice that told every turn of the day: the second model, according to the engine’s log. The mansion was made by the first model.

A Note on the Telling

Daniel Langston is a character in the Lovecraft world, played by Marc, who wrote the account of him that opens this story and then played all nineteen of his turns on 3 October 2026. This story follows what the world kept of that one day: Langston’s own memories, what Marc typed, and the narrator’s account of each turn. His spoken words are in violet. Where the story gives his thoughts, they come from the account Marc wrote, from lines he typed for him, or from what the world remembered of him in his own voice. On eleven of the nineteen turns Marc took the move the page suggested, so what Langston does there is told, and never why. Dr. Vale and Mr. Pike appear only as Langston saw them.

Marc stopped playing him at the end of the convocation, and Langston’s own record ends there. It says nothing of the watcher in the pale mask, the byakhee, whether the traps were set again, whether the students’ copied pages went anywhere, or what happened on Sunday. It disagrees with itself in three places. The first splintering, at 11:28, was of the flags beneath the northern footing, yet at the convocation Pike says the south footing is cracked. The narrator calls Vale’s collapse an afternoon one, though by the clock it came before noon, so the story leaves the word out. And the arrival of the circle is never counted: six were to be summoned and nine to gather, but the record says only “the remaining brethren,” “the rest of the circle,” and “several companions.”
